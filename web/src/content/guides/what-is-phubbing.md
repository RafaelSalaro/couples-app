You are telling your partner about your day. Halfway through the sentence their eyes drop to the screen in their hand. They say "mm-hm". You finish the sentence anyway, a little quieter than you started it.

There is a word for that small moment: **phubbing**. It is everywhere, almost everyone does it, and it matters more than it looks. The good news is that it is also one of the more fixable things in a relationship, as long as you treat it as something to solve together rather than a fault in one of you.

## What phubbing means

Phubbing is a blend of "phone" and "snubbing": attending to your phone instead of the person in front of you. The word was invented in 2012 for a campaign by the Macquarie Dictionary in Australia, which wanted a name for a habit everyone had noticed and nobody could describe. Researchers picked it up soon after, and when it happens between romantic partners they often call it **partner phubbing**. You may also see the word **technoference**, meaning everyday interruptions of couple time by technology.

Phubbing is not using a phone near your partner. Looking up a recipe together, answering a call from your mother or sharing a funny video is not a snub. Phubbing is the moment your partner is trying to connect with you, and the phone wins.

## What it looks like at home

Most phubbing is small and unplanned:

- Glancing at a notification while your partner is talking.
- Scrolling at the dinner table.
- Picking up the phone the second there is a pause in conversation.
- Lying in bed back to back, each lit by a screen.
- Saying "I'm listening" without looking up.
- Reaching for the phone first thing in the morning, before saying good morning.

None of these is dramatic. That is exactly why they add up. Each one is a tiny signal about where your attention would rather be, and partners are very good at reading those signals.

## What the research says

Researchers have been studying phubbing for about a decade. A few findings stand out, with the usual caution that studies describe averages, not your relationship.

**Partner phubbing goes with lower relationship satisfaction.** In 2016, James Roberts and Meredith David at Baylor University developed a scale to measure partner phubbing and surveyed adults in the United States. People who reported more phubbing from their partner also reported more conflict over phone use, and that conflict was linked to lower satisfaction with the relationship [1].

**Everyday interruptions are common.** In a study of women in committed relationships, Brandon McDaniel and Sarah Coyne found that most reported technology interrupting time with their partner, in conversations, at meals and during leisure time. Those who reported more of this "technoference" also reported more conflict about technology and lower relationship satisfaction [2].

**It seems to work through feeling left out.** In an experiment, Varoth Chotpitayasunondh and Karen Douglas showed people an animated conversation and asked them to imagine being one of the two people in it. When the other person was absorbed in a phone, participants rated the conversation as lower in quality and felt less connected, and this was explained partly by a threatened sense of belonging [3]. In plain words: being phubbed feels like being quietly excluded.

**Even a phone on the table may matter.** In experiments by Andrew Przybylski and Netta Weinstein, pairs of strangers who talked with a phone visible nearby reported less closeness and trust than pairs who talked with a notebook there instead, especially when the topic was personally meaningful [4].

**It spreads.** The same research group that ran the animation study found that people who are phubbed are more likely to phub others, which helps explain how it became so normal [5]. If you have ever picked up your phone because your partner picked up theirs, you have felt this.

A fair caveat: most of this work relies on surveys taken at one point in time. It can show that phubbing and unhappiness travel together. It cannot prove which causes which, and it is likely that the arrow points both ways. People also reach for their phones more when a relationship already feels flat.

## Why we do it (it is not a character flaw)

It helps to be honest about what you are up against. The apps on your phone are designed by large teams whose job is to capture as much of your attention as possible. Notifications, endless feeds and little red badges all work on a simple loop: there might be something new, so check. Then check again.

Against that, a quiet moment on the couch does not stand much of a chance. Most phubbing is not a choice to ignore a partner. It is a habit firing in a gap: a pause in conversation, a slow moment, a bit of tiredness or stress.

This matters for how you talk about it. If phubbing is a moral failing, the conversation becomes an accusation. If it is a habit that two people have picked up in a world designed to give it to them, the conversation becomes a project you share.

## How to bring it up kindly

Timing and wording make most of the difference.

1. **Choose a calm moment.** Not while it is happening, and not in the middle of an argument about something else.
2. **Talk about what you miss.** "I miss our talks at dinner" opens a door. "You're always on your phone" closes one.
3. **Include yourself.** Start with your own habit: "I've noticed I grab my phone the moment I sit down." It is almost certainly true, and it makes the conversation equal.
4. **Ask, don't diagnose.** "When does my phone get in your way most?" will teach you something.
5. **Suggest one small experiment.** A week of phone-free dinners is easier to say yes to than a new set of house rules.

## What helps

There is no single fix, but a few changes come up again and again.

**Create phone-free moments, not a phone-free life.** Choose one or two daily moments that belong to the two of you: dinner, the first ten minutes after work, the last half hour before sleep. Our guide to [an evening routine for couples](/guides/evening-routine-for-couples/) has a simple version.

**Give phones a home.** Out of sight really does help. A shelf by the door or a charger outside the bedroom removes a hundred small decisions.

**Narrate the exceptions.** "I need to answer this, it's my sister, two minutes" turns a snub into a shared moment. The phone is the same. The message is the opposite.

**Turn off what you can.** Most notifications are not urgent. Fewer buzzes means fewer glances.

**Pause the most tempting apps in the evening.** If good intentions keep losing to habit, let the phone hold the line for you. Here is how to [pause social media at night on iPhone](/guides/pause-social-media-at-night-iphone/).

**Agree on it together, and keep it equal.** Whatever you try, the same rules should apply to both of you, and either of you should be able to ask for a change. A plan one person imposes on the other tends to create a new problem. Our guide to [screen time agreements for couples](/guides/screen-time-agreement-for-couples/) covers how to set one up.

**Notice the good evenings.** When a phone-free dinner turns into a long talk, say so. People repeat what gets appreciated.

## When it is about more than the phone

Sometimes the phone is a symptom. If one of you is retreating into a screen to avoid conversations, or if every attempt to talk about it turns into a fight, the real subject may be something else: stress, distance, an unresolved hurt. A couples counselor can help with that, and going is a sign of care, not of crisis.
