It is eleven at night. You opened Instagram to check one thing forty minutes ago. You are tired, you know you will regret it tomorrow, and your thumb keeps going.

You do not need a new phone or superhuman discipline. Your iPhone already has tools that pause social apps on a schedule. This guide walks through them step by step, and then deals honestly with the reason they often stop working after a week.

The steps below are for recent versions of iOS. Apple renames things from time to time, so a label may differ slightly on your phone.

## The short version

1. **Settings → Screen Time → Downtime:** schedule it for your evening and night.
2. **Settings → Screen Time → Always Allowed:** choose the apps that stay available.
3. **Settings → Screen Time → App Limits:** optionally add a daily limit for Social.
4. **Settings → Focus → Sleep:** silence notifications overnight.
5. **Add a little friction** so one tap does not undo it all.

## Step 1: Schedule Downtime for the evening

Downtime is the most useful setting for nights. Between the hours you choose, only phone calls and the apps you allow remain available. Everything else is dimmed on your Home Screen with a small hourglass.

1. Open **Settings** and tap **Screen Time**. If you have never used it, turn on **App & Website Activity** when asked.
2. Tap **Downtime**.
3. Turn on **Scheduled**.
4. Choose **Every Day**, or **Customize Days** if you want different hours on weekends.
5. Set **From** and **To**. For example, from 9:00 PM to 7:00 AM.

You will get a reminder five minutes before Downtime starts.

A tip on choosing hours: be generous. Most late-night scrolling starts earlier than we think. If you want your evenings back and not only your sleep, start Downtime at dinner time rather than at bedtime.

## Step 2: Choose what stays available

Downtime pauses everything except what you allow, so take a minute to make that list sensible.

1. In **Screen Time**, tap **Always Allowed**.
2. Under **Allowed Apps**, keep the things you really need at night: Phone, Messages, Maps, your alarm, your banking app, music or a meditation app.
3. Remove anything that tends to swallow your evening.
4. Under **Contacts**, you can choose who can reach you during Downtime.

The goal is a phone that still works as a phone, a map and an alarm clock, and nothing more.

## Step 3: Add App Limits if you want a daily cap

App Limits are different from Downtime. Instead of following the clock, they count how long you have used something that day.

1. In **Screen Time**, tap **App Limits**, then **Add Limit**.
2. Tick **Social** to include the whole category, or tap it to choose individual apps such as Instagram, TikTok or X.
3. Tap **Next**, set the time, for example 45 minutes a day, and tap **Add**.
4. To include a website, scroll to the bottom of the app list and add it under **Websites**.

When the time is used up, the app shows a limit screen until midnight.

You can use Downtime, App Limits or both. For nights, Downtime does most of the work. A daily limit is a good addition if daytime scrolling bothers you too.

## Step 4: Use Sleep Focus for quiet nights

Downtime handles the apps. Focus handles the interruptions.

1. Open **Settings → Focus → Sleep**. If you do not see it, set up a sleep schedule in the **Health** app first, under **Browse → Sleep**.
2. Set your bedtime and wake-up time.
3. Choose which people and apps may still notify you. Calls from favorites is a common choice.

With Sleep Focus on, your Lock Screen dims and notifications wait until morning. Fewer buzzes mean fewer reasons to pick the phone up in the first place.

## Step 5: Make it harder to undo

Here is the honest part. When you open a paused app, iPhone shows a limit screen with an **Ignore Limit** button. Tap it and you are offered **One More Minute**, **Remind Me in 15 Minutes** or **Ignore Limit For Today**. At eleven at night, with a tired brain, that last option is very tempting.

A few ways to add friction:

- **Set a Screen Time passcode.** In **Screen Time**, tap **Lock Screen Time Settings** and choose a four-digit code that is different from your phone's passcode. With a passcode set, you can make Downtime firm, so that getting more time needs the code rather than a single tap.
- **Move the apps off your first Home Screen,** or remove them from the Home Screen altogether so they live only in the App Library. Out of sight helps more than you would think.
- **Log out of the worst offender each evening.** Typing a password is often enough of a pause to change your mind.
- **Charge the phone outside the bedroom.** A simple alarm clock costs very little and removes the main reason the phone is next to your pillow.
- **Turn the screen gray at night.** In **Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters**, choose **Grayscale**. A colorless feed is far less interesting.

## Why your own limits are easy to ignore

Even with all of this, many people find their limits fade within a couple of weeks. It is not a weakness. It is a design problem: **the person setting the limit and the person tempted to ignore it are the same person**, and the tempted one knows the passcode.

Limits that hold usually involve someone else. That is why one common piece of advice is to ask a person you trust to set your Screen Time passcode and keep it.

## Doing it together with your partner

If you live with a partner, the natural person is right there. A few ways to do it that keep things fair:

- **Hold each other's passcodes.** Each of you sets the code on the other's phone. When one of you wants more time, you ask. It is a small moment of honesty, and often a nice excuse to talk.
- **Choose the same hours.** If both phones go quiet at the same time, nobody is sitting alone next to someone who is scrolling.
- **Keep it equal.** The same rules for both of you, agreed together, and either of you can ask to change them. One partner policing the other is a recipe for resentment, not for better evenings.
- **Keep it private.** Your partner does not need to see what you do on your phone, and you do not need to see theirs. Screen Time's family features are designed for parents managing a child's device. They are not a good fit for two equal adults.
