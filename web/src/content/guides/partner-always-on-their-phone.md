You are talking, and you can see the exact moment you lose them. Their eyes drop, their thumb moves, and whatever you were saying trails off. After enough evenings like that, a thought settles in: *I am less interesting than that screen.*

If that is where you are, this guide is for you. It will not give you a trick to make your partner put the phone down. It offers something more useful: a way to understand what is happening, a way to talk about it that tends to work, and a plan that the two of you can try together.

## First: it is probably not about you

It feels personal. It almost never is.

Phones are built to hold attention. Every feed is endless on purpose, and every notification is a small invitation to come back. Against that, a tired person on a couch has very little defense. Most of the time your partner is not choosing the phone over you. They are not choosing at all. A habit is running.

There are also plain reasons people sink into a screen in the evening:

- **They are worn out,** and scrolling asks nothing of them.
- **Work follows them home** through email and messages.
- **They are stressed or low,** and the phone is a way to not think for a while.
- **It is how they stay in touch** with friends and family.
- **Nothing else is on offer.** If the default evening is two people and two screens, the phone fills the space.

None of this makes the lonely feeling less real. But it changes what you are dealing with. You do not have a partner who does not care. You have a habit, and habits can be changed.

## Second: take an honest look at yourself

This part is uncomfortable, and it is the one that makes everything else easier.

For a few days, notice your own phone. When do you pick it up without deciding to? Do you ever reach for it because your partner reached for theirs? Most people who feel ignored discover that they also do a fair amount of ignoring. That is not a reason to drop the subject. It is the best possible way into it, because "we" is far easier to hear than "you".

## Third: notice when it hurts most

"Always" is hard to act on. Specific moments are not. Over a week, notice when your partner's phone bothers you most. Common answers:

- At the dinner table.
- In the first minutes after one of you gets home.
- In bed, before sleep or first thing in the morning.
- When you are in the middle of telling them something.
- On days off, when there was time to do something together.

You will probably find that it is two or three moments, not the whole day. Those moments are what you will ask for.

## How to bring it up

Timing and wording decide how this goes.

**Pick a calm moment.** Not while it is happening and not during another argument. A walk is ideal.

**Start with what you miss.** "I miss talking with you at dinner." "I love our evenings when we actually hang out, and I want more of them." This is an invitation, and people respond to invitations.

**Own your part.** "I've noticed I'm on my phone a lot too."

**Ask before you propose.** "How do you feel about how we spend our evenings?" You may hear something you did not expect: that they are exhausted, that they thought you wanted quiet, that they have felt the same.

**Then suggest one small experiment.** "Could we try phones away at dinner for a week and see how it feels?"

What to avoid: "you always" and "you never", comparisons with other couples, bringing up the screen time number as proof, and raising it in the heat of the moment. Each of these turns a shared problem into a trial, and nobody relaxes in a trial.

## A plan to try together

If your partner is open to it, keep the first step small and equal.

1. **Choose one protected moment a day.** Dinner is the easiest. Both phones go in the same place, out of reach, for that time.
2. **Agree on exceptions.** If one of you is expecting a call, say so. A quick "I need to take this" keeps an exception from feeling like a snub.
3. **Put something in the space.** A question each, a card game, a walk after dinner. An empty silence sends people straight back to their phones. Our list of [phone-free date ideas](/guides/phone-free-date-ideas/) will help.
4. **Make it easier with settings.** Fewer notifications, phones charging outside the bedroom, and social apps paused in the evening. Here is how to [pause social media at night on iPhone](/guides/pause-social-media-at-night-iphone/).
5. **Check in after a week.** What was nice? What was hard? What should change?

If it goes well, add a second moment. For a fuller version, see our guide to making a [screen time agreement as a couple](/guides/screen-time-agreement-for-couples/).

Two things matter more than the details. **Keep it mutual:** the same rules for both of you, decided together. And **keep it kind:** when one of you slips, a light "phone?" and a smile does more than a sigh ever will.

## If your partner is not interested

Sometimes the answer is "I don't think it's a problem" or "I need my phone to unwind". That is frustrating. You still have options.

- **Go first.** Put your own phone away during the moments you care about, without announcing it or keeping score. People often follow what they see.
- **Make an offer instead of a request.** "Want to go for a walk after dinner?" is easier to say yes to than "can you put your phone down?"
- **Ask for one small thing.** Not a new way of living. Just dinner, or just the first ten minutes after work.
- **Say how it feels, once, clearly.** "When we are together and you're on your phone, I feel alone. I'd love more of your attention." Then leave room for them to think about it.

What does not work is monitoring, nagging or taking their phone. You cannot manage another adult into being present. Trying to tends to push them further away, and it is not the kind of relationship either of you wants.

## When it is a sign of something else

Occasionally the phone is not the problem but the place a problem is hiding. Pay attention if:

- your partner uses the phone to avoid every difficult conversation;
- there is secrecy that worries you;
- one of you seems persistently low, anxious or withdrawn; or
- attempts to talk about it always end in a fight.

In those cases the real subject is bigger than screen time, and a couples counselor can help you talk about it safely. Reaching out is a sign that you care about the relationship.
