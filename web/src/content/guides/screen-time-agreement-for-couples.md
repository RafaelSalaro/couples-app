Most couples have had some version of this conversation. One says, "You're always on your phone." The other says, "So are you." Both are a little bit right, both feel a little bit attacked, and nothing changes.

A screen time agreement is a way to have that conversation once, calmly, and end up with something you both chose. It is not a contract and it is not a set of rules one partner writes for the other. It is a few sentences about how the two of you want your time together to feel.

This guide walks through how to make one, with a template at the end.

## What a good agreement is, and is not

**It is mutual.** The same commitments apply to both of you. If phones are away at dinner, both phones are away at dinner.

**It is written together.** Each of you gets to say what matters to you and what would be hard. Nobody arrives with a finished document.

**It is about time together, not about each other's phones.** The subject is "our evenings", not "your Instagram".

**It is easy to change.** Either of you can ask to revisit it at any time. A good agreement gets adjusted often in the first month.

**It is not surveillance.** You do not need to see each other's screen time numbers, messages or apps. If an agreement only works when one of you is checking on the other, it is not working.

**It is not a punishment system.** There are no fines and no scorekeeping. If a day goes badly, you start again the next day.

Keeping to these points is what separates an agreement that brings you closer from one that becomes another thing to argue about.

## Before you talk: notice your own habits

Spend a few days paying attention to your own phone use before you bring anything up. Not to feel guilty, just to be accurate.

- When do you reach for your phone without deciding to?
- Which moments with your partner does it interrupt most?
- Which apps leave you feeling worse afterwards?
- What would you like more of in your evenings?

On iPhone, **Settings → Screen Time** shows your own daily and weekly use. Keep those numbers for yourself. They are a mirror, not evidence.

Arriving with "here is what I've noticed about me" changes the whole tone of the conversation.

## Step 1: Start the conversation well

Pick a relaxed moment. A walk is ideal, because walking side by side makes hard topics easier. Avoid starting right after an irritating phone moment.

Some openers that tend to go well:

- "I've been thinking about how we spend our evenings. Can we talk about it?"
- "I've noticed I'm on my phone more than I want to be when we're together. I'd like to change that, and I'd love to do it with you."
- "I miss talking with you at dinner. Could we try something for a week?"

And some to avoid: anything starting with "you always" or "you never", and anything that sounds like a verdict.

Then ask what your partner has noticed, and listen. You may hear things about your own habits that are not comfortable. Take them as information.

## Step 2: Agree on what you want more of

Before deciding what to put away, decide what you are making room for. This is the step most couples skip, and it is the one that makes an agreement feel like a gift instead of a restriction.

Each of you finish these sentences:

- "In the evenings I'd love more…"
- "I feel closest to you when…"
- "One thing I miss doing with you is…"

Write the answers down. They are the reason for everything else.

## Step 3: Choose your protected moments

Now pick the times when phones are away for both of you. Start small. One or two moments is plenty for the first week.

Common choices:

- **Meals together.** The most popular starting point, because it has a clear beginning and end.
- **The first fifteen minutes after you are both home.**
- **The bedroom.** Phones charge elsewhere overnight.
- **A set evening window,** such as 8 to 10 PM.
- **Dates and walks.**

Be specific. "Less phone in the evenings" is a wish. "Phones on the hallway shelf from dinner until 9" is an agreement.

## Step 4: Decide how exceptions work

Real life has exceptions: a work deadline, a sick parent, a friend who needs you. An agreement with no room for them will not last a week.

Agree on three things:

1. **What counts.** Calls from family, being on call for work, anything urgent. You do not need a complete list, just a shared sense.
2. **How to flag it.** A quick "I need to take this, back in five" is enough. Saying it out loud is what keeps an exception from feeling like a snub.
3. **How to ask for time.** If one of you wants to use a paused app during protected time, you simply say so. The other can say "go for it" or "can it wait until after dinner?" Either answer is fine, and neither needs a debate.

Treat asking as a normal, friendly thing rather than a request for a favor. You are two adults keeping each other company in a habit, not one person supervising the other.

## Step 5: Decide what happens after a slip

You will both slip. Decide now how to handle it, while it is still hypothetical.

What works: a light, agreed signal. One word ("phone?"), a raised eyebrow, a hand held out. Then carry on with your evening.

What does not work: sighing, counting, bringing up last Tuesday. Shame makes people defensive, and defensive people hide their phones rather than put them down.

It also helps to celebrate the good days more than you mention the missed ones. "I really liked tonight" does more for a habit than any reminder.

## Step 6: Make it easy to keep

Good intentions are weakest at 9 PM. Let your surroundings do some of the work.

- **Give the phones a home** outside the rooms where you spend time together.
- **Turn off non-urgent notifications.**
- **Use your phone's own tools.** On iPhone, Screen Time can pause chosen apps on a schedule. Our guide shows how to [pause social media at night on iPhone](/guides/pause-social-media-at-night-iphone/).
- **Hold the line for each other.** Some couples set each other's Screen Time passcode, so that getting extra time means asking. If you do this, do it both ways.

## Step 7: Review it together

Put a short check-in in the calendar after one week, and then once a month. Three questions are enough:

1. What has been good?
2. What has been hard?
3. What should we change?

Expect to adjust. Maybe 8 PM was too early, or dinners are easy and bedtime is the real challenge. Loosening or tightening the agreement is a decision for both of you, made together.

## A template you can copy

Write your own version by hand and put it somewhere you both see it.

> **Our agreement**
>
> We want more: _unhurried dinners, walks, time to talk before sleep._
>
> **Our protected time:** _every day from dinner until 9 PM, and phones stay out of the bedroom overnight._
>
> **During protected time:** _both phones live on the hallway shelf. Calls from family and anything urgent are always fine. We say so out loud._
>
> **If one of us wants to use a paused app:** _we ask each other. "Go for it" and "let's wait" are both good answers._
>
> **If one of us slips:** _the other says "phone?" and we move on. No scorekeeping._
>
> **We will check in:** _next Sunday, then on the first Sunday of each month. Either of us can ask to change this at any time._

## Keep it equal

One last point, because it matters most. An agreement like this should make both of you feel more free, not less. Watch for these signs that it has drifted:

- One of you makes the rules and the other follows them.
- One of you checks the other's phone or usage.
- Saying no to a rule does not feel safe.

If any of those is true, stop and talk, and if needed set the agreement aside. Time together only means something when both people are choosing it.
