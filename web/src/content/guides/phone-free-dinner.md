If you want to spend less time on your phones and more time with each other, dinner is the place to start. It happens every day. It has a clear beginning and end. You are already sitting down together. And it is short enough that nobody feels they are giving anything up.

This guide covers how to set up a phone-free dinner, what to do with the quiet, and how to keep the habit going after the first enthusiastic week.

## Why dinner is the best first habit

Most advice about phones asks for a lot: fewer hours, more discipline, a new morning routine. Dinner asks for twenty to forty minutes that you were going to spend at the table anyway.

It also gives a lot back. In one field experiment, researchers asked groups of friends and family sharing a restaurant meal either to keep their phones on the table or to put them away. The groups with phones out reported feeling more distracted and enjoyed the meal slightly less [1]. The difference was small, and that is the point: a small improvement, every day, for almost no effort.

There is a simpler reason too. For many couples, dinner is the only moment in a working day when they look at each other for more than a few seconds.

## Step 1: Agree on it together

A phone-free dinner that one person announces and the other puts up with will not last. Bring it up as an idea, not a rule.

"I'd love to try dinners without our phones for a week. Would you be up for that?"

Then agree on the details together:

- **Which meals?** Every dinner, weeknights only, or just the dinners you eat together at home.
- **When does it start and end?** A good version: from sitting down until the table is cleared.
- **Does it include the television?** Many couples find that switching it off for the meal makes the biggest difference. Decide what suits you.

The same rule applies to both of you. That is what makes it feel like something you are doing together rather than something being done to one of you.

## Step 2: Give the phones a home

Where the phones go matters more than anything else in this guide.

- **Good:** a shelf by the door, a drawer, a basket in the hallway, a charger in another room.
- **Not good enough:** face down on the table, in a pocket, on the kitchen counter within reach.

If the phone can be reached without standing up, sooner or later it will be. Put both phones in the same place at the same time. It becomes a tiny ritual: phones down, plates out.

Leave the sound on if either of you needs to be reachable for calls. Turn off or silence everything else.

## Step 3: Have something to talk about

The first few dinners can feel oddly quiet. That is normal. You have removed something that filled every pause, and it takes a few evenings to find your rhythm again. A little structure helps.

**Rose, thorn and bud.** Each person shares the best part of their day, the hardest part, and something they are looking forward to.

**One thing I haven't told you yet.** There is always something: a conversation at work, a thought on the way home, something funny you saw.

**A question jar.** Spend ten minutes one weekend writing questions on slips of paper. Pull one out each night. Some to start with:

- What is a small thing that made you happy this week?
- What did you love doing when you were ten?
- Where would you go if we had a free weekend and no plans?
- What is something you have changed your mind about?
- What is a meal you remember from childhood?
- What are you looking forward to this month?
- What is something I do that makes your day easier?
- If we could learn one thing together, what would it be?

**Plan something.** The weekend, a trip, a meal you want to cook.

And sometimes, say nothing much. Eating quietly together, with nothing buzzing, is its own kind of company.

## Step 4: Decide how exceptions work

Life will interrupt. Agree in advance how to handle it, so that an exception does not feel like the end of the habit.

- **Expecting a call:** say so before sitting down. If it rings, answer it.
- **Need to look something up:** write it down and look after dinner. Most questions can wait twenty minutes, and guessing is more fun.
- **On call for work:** keep the phone on loud in the next room.
- **Eating alone:** the habit is for shared meals. Do what you like.
- **Eating out:** same idea. Phones stay in a bag or pocket until the bill arrives.

## Step 5: Know what to do when it slips

It will. One of you will bring a phone to the table out of pure habit. This is the moment that decides whether the habit survives.

What works is a light, agreed signal: "phone?" with a smile, or simply holding out a hand to carry it to the shelf. Then carry on with dinner.

What does not work is a sigh, a lecture or keeping count. Nobody keeps doing something that makes them feel told off.

If you miss a night, or a week, nothing is lost. Start again at the next dinner.

## Making it last

**Tie it to the table, not to willpower.** The habit is "when we sit down to eat, the phones go on the shelf". Linking it to an action you already do every day is what makes it automatic.

**Make dinner itself a bit nicer.** A candle, a tablecloth, music. None of it is necessary, but small signals that this time is different help the habit feel like a treat.

**Cook together when you can.** The conversation starts at the stove and carries on at the table.

**Notice it out loud.** "That was a nice dinner" is all it takes.

**Grow it slowly.** When dinner feels natural, extend it: a walk afterwards, or phones staying on the shelf until the kitchen is tidy. Our [evening routine for couples](/guides/evening-routine-for-couples/) shows how dinner can become the anchor for the whole evening.

## With children at the table

Everything above applies, with two additions.

**The rule is for everyone.** Children notice immediately when adults exempt themselves. If the phones are away, all the phones are away.

**Let them own part of it.** Children can decorate the phone basket, pick the question from the jar, or be in charge of reminding the adults. Teenagers are more likely to join in if they helped set the terms, including how long it lasts.
