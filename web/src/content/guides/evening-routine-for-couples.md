Most couples see each other for only a few waking hours on a weekday. Those hours usually arrive when you are both tired, hungry and carrying the day with you. It is no surprise that they often dissolve into dinner in front of a screen, some separate scrolling and a mumbled goodnight.

An evening routine is not a schedule and it is not another thing to be good at. It is a handful of small moments that happen in the same order most nights, so that being together stops depending on how much energy either of you has left.

Here is a version that works for a lot of couples. Take what fits and leave the rest.

## Why evenings slip away

Three things work against you between six and eleven.

**You are running on empty.** By evening most people have spent their focus and patience for the day. The easiest thing wins, and nothing is easier than a phone.

**There is no boundary.** Work messages, news and group chats follow you home. Without a clear moment when the day ends, it never quite does.

**Parallel time feels like together time.** You are in the same room, so it seems like you are spending the evening together. But sitting next to each other on separate screens is not the same as being with each other, and most of us can feel the difference.

A routine helps with all three. It replaces decisions with defaults, it marks the end of the working day, and it turns a little of that parallel time into shared time.

## The routine: five small moments

### 1. A real hello (2 minutes)

When the second person gets home, stop what you are doing. Stand up, make eye contact, hug for a moment longer than usual. Ask one question and listen to the answer.

It sounds almost too small to matter. But the first minute sets the tone for the whole evening. A hello called over a shoulder from behind a screen says "I'm busy". A real one says "I'm glad you're here".

If you work from home, create an arrival anyway: close the laptop, change your clothes or step outside for two minutes, then say hello.

### 2. Park the phones (1 minute)

Choose a home for both phones that is not the couch, the table or a pocket. A shelf by the door or a charger in the hallway works well. Sound on for calls if you need to be reachable.

This one step does most of the work. The other moments are much easier when the phone is across the room rather than within reach. If you both want help keeping to it, you can set your phones to pause social apps for the evening. ### 3. Eat together, screens off (20 to 40 minutes)

Sit at a table if you have one. It does not need to be a cooked-from-scratch meal. Takeaway on real plates is fine.

If conversation feels rusty, borrow a structure:

- **Rose, thorn and bud:** the best part of your day, the hard part, and one thing you are looking forward to.
- **One thing I haven't told you yet.** There is always something.
- **A question jar.** Write twenty questions on slips of paper one Sunday and pull one out each night.

Cooking together counts double. One chops, one stirs, and the conversation mostly takes care of itself.

### 4. Do one thing together (20 minutes or more)

This is the part that makes an evening feel like an evening. Pick something low-effort that you both enjoy:

- A walk after dinner
- A card or board game
- A puzzle that lives on the coffee table
- A show you chose together, watched without phones
- Reading on the same couch
- Music and tidying the kitchen side by side

Keep a short list on the fridge so you never have to think of something when you are tired. Our list of [phone-free date ideas](/guides/phone-free-date-ideas/) has 45 to choose from.

Time apart in the evening is healthy too. One of you might go to the gym or call a friend. The routine is not about spending every minute together. It is about making sure some minutes are fully shared.

### 5. Wind down together (10 minutes)

In the last stretch before sleep, keep things quiet and close:

- Leave the phones charging outside the bedroom, or at least across the room. An inexpensive alarm clock removes the last excuse.
- Share one good thing about the day or one thing you appreciated about each other. It takes thirty seconds and it is a lovely last thought to fall asleep on.
- Read, stretch or simply talk in the dark.

Many people also find they fall asleep more easily when the last half hour of the day is spent away from a bright, busy screen.

## The 10-minute version for hard days

Some nights there is no time or energy. Keep the skeleton:

1. A real hello.
2. Phones parked for ten minutes.
3. Sit down together with a drink and ask, "What was today like?"

That is it. Ten minutes of full attention on a bad day does more than you would expect, and it keeps the habit alive until the next good day.

## If you have kids

Evenings with children are a relay race. The routine still works, in a different shape:

- Make dinner and bedtime a shared, phone-free stretch for the whole family.
- Protect the first twenty minutes after the children are asleep for the two of you, before chores and before screens. Tea on the couch is enough.
- Take turns. If one of you handles bedtime, the other clears the kitchen, so you both arrive at the couch at the same time.

## If your schedules do not match

Shift work, late meetings and different sleep patterns are real. Look for the overlap and guard it. For some couples the "evening" routine happens at breakfast. For others it is a fifteen-minute call during a break and a longer evening on days off. The pattern matters more than the hour.

## Making it stick

**Start with one moment, not five.** The real hello or phone-free dinner is a good first step. Add the next one when the first feels normal.

**Agree on it together.** A routine one person designs and the other puts up with will not last. Talk about which parts each of you wants, and keep the same rules for both. Our guide to [screen time agreements for couples](/guides/screen-time-agreement-for-couples/) can help with the conversation.

**Expect missed days.** You will skip nights. Everyone does. A missed evening is just a missed evening, and nobody needs to feel bad about it. Start again the next day.

**Notice when it works.** "That was a nice evening" is worth saying out loud. It is how a routine turns into something you both look forward to.
